# The Human Function World Model and Medicine: Human Function Exists Independently of Medicine, and Medicine Serves Human Function

Human function was not created by medicine.

Before medicine existed, the human body was already maintaining respiration and circulation, regulating its internal environment, resisting threats, limiting damage, repairing tissue, recovering, and adapting. People also needed to eat, move, communicate, work, care for themselves, and participate in life.

Medicine is a knowledge and professional-action system created by human beings. It helps us understand disease, injury, bodily structure, physiological and pathological processes, and risk. Through prevention, diagnosis, medication, surgery, monitoring, and other forms of care, medicine changes the conditions under which the body operates.

Medicine ultimately serves the person. Human function is not the whole person, but it is a major bodily foundation for sustaining life, recovering, and living in the world.

The relationship between medicine and human function is therefore **asymmetric in purpose and bidirectional in operation**:

> In purpose, medicine serves the person and the person’s human function.  
> In operation, medicine continuously changes human function, while human function continuously provides medicine with questions, goals, boundaries, and feedback.

## What is human function?

Human function can be understood simply as:

> **The body’s capacity to be appropriately engaged to meet internal and external demands.**

It contains two aspects that must not be collapsed into one another.

The first is **functional capacity**: what the body can provide, to what level, for how long, and within what boundary.

The second is **actual engagement**: when a concrete demand arises, whether and how the body’s available capacities are actually organized and expressed.

Having a capacity does not guarantee that it will be appropriately expressed in a particular situation. Completing a task also does not prove that the task was completed at acceptable cost or within a safe boundary.

Human-function reasoning therefore starts with what the body must accomplish.

Internally, the body must maintain, regulate, defend, repair, and recover. Externally, the person must eat, move, communicate, work, exercise, and participate in life. These tasks generate demands. The next questions are which human-function capacities are required, how much capacity is currently available, and how those capacities are actually engaged.

Human function is therefore not limited to walking, movement, or exercise performance.

It also includes homeostatic and internal physiological regulation, protection and defense, recovery and adaptation, sleep–wake regulation, cognition and executive function, emotional and behavioral regulation, communication, intake, and swallowing protection.

## What is medicine?

Medicine studies and manages human health, disease, and injury.

It asks what the condition is, why it occurred, how serious it is, what risks it creates, and whether examination, treatment, observation, reassessment, or another medical action is required.

A physician may directly confront infection, cancer, fracture, bleeding, vascular disease, organ pathology, abnormal physiological processes, or an acute medical emergency.

Medication may control infection, inflammation, blood pressure, or other pathological changes. Surgery may remove disease, repair structure, relieve compression, or reconstruct conditions. Medical devices may support, compensate for, or temporarily substitute for some bodily functions.

But disease, lesions, measurements, medication, and surgery are not the final ends of medicine.

Medical action must ultimately return to a concrete person:

Did it protect life? Did it reduce harm? Did it reduce suffering? Did it preserve, restore, or support human function? Did it help the person continue living?

When full restoration is not possible, medicine may still preserve remaining capacity, reduce suffering, support dignity, and improve life.

## From medicine to human function

Disease, injury, and medical action all affect human function.

Disease may create additional internal tasks, reduce functional capacity, disrupt the actual engagement of available capacities, increase bodily burden, prolong recovery, and make previously manageable work, self-care, mobility, or exercise more difficult.

Medical action can control threats, change pathological processes, repair structure, reduce risk, and create conditions for recovery.

But medical treatment does not necessarily improve every function immediately.

Surgery may temporarily increase the body’s repair burden. Immobilization may temporarily reduce mobility and load-bearing capacity. Medication or other treatments may affect fatigue, appetite, cognition, or task tolerance.

At times, medicine may also accept a temporary loss of one function in order to protect life, control a greater risk, or create a better long-term recovery opportunity.

Medical action should therefore not be evaluated only by whether a treatment was completed. It should also ask:

> What did this medical action change in this person’s capacity, actual engagement, recovery, cost, boundary, and life?

## From human function back to medicine

Human function also feeds back into medicine.

First, it shows what a disease actually means for this person.

The same diagnosis, similar imaging, or comparable test results can have very different real-world consequences in different people.

One person may continue to live stably. Another may still complete tasks but at steadily increasing cost. Another may be losing the ability to work, move, or care for themselves. One person may recover as expected while another experiences prolonged or abnormal recovery.

These human-function facts can help medicine clarify treatment goals, understand the person-level meaning of disease, and evaluate whether treatment has produced a meaningful change in the person.

Second, longitudinal human-function change can generate a new medical question.

A rapid decline in previously stable walking, persistent unexplained weakness, markedly delayed recovery, progressively reduced task tolerance, or a new pattern that the existing functional explanation can no longer explain may indicate the need for medical reassessment.

Such changes are not diagnoses by themselves. They can, however, become risk signals, reasons for further evaluation, and governed triggers for medical re-entry.

Human-function evidence may also contribute information about the impact of disease on life, current recovery, and personal goals when medication, surgery, observation, or another medical path is being considered. It does not replace medical indications, risk–benefit analysis, or the final medical decision.

Two boundaries must always remain clear:

> **Medical clearance does not mean that human function has fully recovered.**  
> **The absence of an obvious functional problem does not mean that medical risk is absent.**

## The body also participates in disease response and recovery

The body is not a passive object waiting to be treated.

After disease, infection, injury, or surgery, the body must perform internal tasks such as defense, hemostasis and damage control, internal regulation, tissue protection and repair, restoration of homeostasis, recovery, and adaptation.

Disease, infection, injury, and surgery are not themselves those internal tasks. They may trigger the tasks and create new demands on the body.

What is often called “self-healing” is not a mysterious single force. More precisely, it is the result of multiple human functions and physiological processes involved in defense, regulation, repair, recovery, and adaptation.

Medicine may control infection, remove pathology, repair structure, reduce risk, support recovery conditions, and, when necessary, support, compensate for, or temporarily substitute for some bodily functions.

But many processes of defense, regulation, tissue repair, recovery, and adaptation still have to be performed by the body itself.

Medicine therefore does not replace human function.

> **By understanding and treating disease, medicine creates conditions in which human function can continue, recover, and operate.**

## Why is a Human Function World Model needed?

Human function is an objective reality of living bodies.

The Human Function World Model is not human function itself, and it does not create human function. It is a model that continuously represents and updates a specific person’s human-function state, medical state, actions, feedback, and change over time.

HFWM keeps the **same whole person** as the persistent modeled entity and uses human function as an organizing coordinate. It connects disease, injury, bodily structure, physiological processes, internal tasks, external life tasks, functional capacity, actual engagement, recovery, environment, professional action, and longitudinal history.

These things are not collapsed into one type.

Disease remains disease. Imaging remains imaging. Medication and surgery retain their medical identity. A human-function state does not become a medical diagnosis. The model explains how these distinct facts relate within the same person while preserving their evidence, authority, and scope boundaries.

HFWM continuously asks:

> What state is this person in now?  
> How are medical state and human-function state affecting one another?  
> What internal and external demands is the body currently facing?  
> What is admissible now, and what is not?  
> What changed after an action?  
> Should the current direction continue, or should medicine re-enter?

Medical records are often organized around an appointment, admission, operation, or treatment episode. The person’s body does not stop changing when one medical episode ends.

HFWM follows the same person before disease, during disease, through treatment, during recovery and return to life, and when new changes occur later.

It is not an autonomous diagnostic system and does not replace physicians, medication, surgery, or regulated medical judgment. Its value is to prevent medical facts, the body’s own recovery, real life, and longitudinal change from becoming disconnected.

## Medicine ultimately returns to the same person

Human function already exists independently of medicine.

Medicine did not create it. Medicine is a professional system created to understand disease, protect life, control risk, reduce harm, and help the body recover.

Medicine changes human function. The body’s capacities for defense, regulation, repair, recovery, and adaptation also influence disease and recovery. Human-function change, in turn, continuously provides medicine with new questions, goals, boundaries, and outcome feedback.

Medicine and human function are therefore not two parallel worlds.

> **Medicine ultimately serves the person.**  
> **At the bodily level, medicine serves human function.**

The Human Function World Model keeps the same person continuous across medicine, the body’s own recovery, real life, and change over time, so that each medical action can ultimately return to the person and the human function it is meant to serve.
