# 4. Definition and Core Commitments of HFWM

| Definition. A Human Function World Model is a whole-person world model that continuously represents and reasons over the same person across internal and external demands, represents possible state transitions, selects governed actions, and updates the person-specific model over time. In its current operational profile, governed internal/external task representations provide runtime entries that generate or trace to Demand. Human function is the organizing coordinate connecting bodily structure and physiology, disease and injury, functional capacity, Actual Engagement Processes and engagement assertions, manifestations, cost, boundaries, recovery, evidence, professional and life context, action, and change over time. Calibrated future-state prediction is a staged empirical capability rather than an assumed property of every implementation. |
|------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|

The definition contains four commitments. First, the modeled entity is
the whole person, not an organ, disease, test result, profession, or
isolated activity. Second, human function is the organizing center, not
the boundary of the modeled world. Third, in the current operational
profile, governed internal and external task representations are
first-class antecedents of Demand; the published UOHF root semantics
remain demand-centered. Fourth, state, evidence, inference, action,
feedback, and model update remain distinct so that the system does not
confuse reality with representation.

HFWM therefore does not reduce the person to function. Social identity,
values, preferences, relationships, and other dimensions of personhood
are not redefined as human function. Instead, human function provides a
unified operational view of how the body sustains life and allows the
person to act in the world under those conditions.

The root UOHF definition remains unchanged: human function is the body's
capacity to be appropriately engaged to meet internal and external
demands [1-3]. HFWM uses that definition as its organizing invariant
while adding whole-person state, history, dynamics, coordination,
action, and longitudinal model update.

Compatibility note. UOHF Definition 2.1 defines Demand as the
upper-level requirement object and treats Task primarily as an
operational specialization of external demand. Later UOHF runtime
governance introduced governed Internal Task and External Task task
types to provide a single operational entry before Demand. HFWM adopts
that runtime convention as an execution profile only: it does not
redefine Demand, does not make a disease, symptom, assessment, service
workflow, or movement template into a task, and does not rewrite the
published UOHF 2.1 root semantics.

# 5. The Whole Person as the Persistent Modeled Entity

Let p denote one person. HFWM requires every person-specific assertion
to remain bound to p across time, professional settings, and data
sources. A laboratory value, imaging finding, movement observation,
dietary history, sleep report, work limitation, surgical episode,
family-support condition, and exercise response may all be relevant, but
they remain different typed facts about the same person rather than
competing definitions of that person.

A latent or modeled whole-person state X_t may include multiscale
physiological, structural, cognitive, behavioral, medical,
environmental, and recovery dimensions [3]. The corresponding
evidence-supported representation is necessarily incomplete. HFWM
therefore maintains a distinction between the person's reality and the
current model of that reality.

# 6. Internal and External Tasks

## 6.1 External tasks

External tasks include self-care, eating, mobility, communication,
learning, work, caregiving, social participation, exercise, sport, and
adaptation to environmental events. The same nominal task generates
different demands under different load, duration, speed, precision,
environment, assistance, and life-stage conditions.

## 6.2 Internal tasks

Internal tasks are governed operational representations of internal
biological goals or challenges that, when legitimately activated,
generate or specify internal Demand. The Demand - not the task - is the
requirement that must be met through the body's organization.
Internal-task identities may cover maintenance of life, regulation of
the internal environment, defense, hemostasis, damage control, repair,
restoration of homeostasis, recovery, adaptation, sleep-wake regulation,
growth, development, and reproduction. Disease and injury are not
themselves internal tasks; they may serve as trigger or context for a
governed internal-task activation. In an operational system, that
activation should depend on a governed identity and rule rather than
keyword inference from a diagnosis or symptom.

This distinction is crucial for modeling self-healing and recovery. An
infection may generate internal tasks for defense, immune regulation,
thermal regulation, energy allocation, tissue protection, and recovery.
A traumatic injury may generate tasks for hemostasis, damage control,
protection, repair, remodeling, and restoration of usable capacity. A
surgical episode may create both internal recovery tasks and new
external constraints on mobility, self-care, communication, eating,
work, or exercise.

## 6.3 Coupling between internal and external tasks

Internal and external tasks are coupled. Exercise is an external task
that generates internal demands for circulation, ventilation,
thermoregulation, energy provision, and recovery. Infection begins as an
internal challenge but may reduce the capacity to work, walk,
communicate, or participate socially. HFWM therefore requires a task set
rather than a single isolated task when the person's current state
depends on multiple simultaneous demands.

```text
TASK internal / external
→ DEMAND
→ REQUIRED CAPACITY
→ CURRENT CAPACITY
→ ACTUAL ENGAGEMENT PROCESS
→ OBSERVED RESPONSE / COST / BOUNDARY / RECOVERY
→ EVIDENCE / ASSERTION / INFERENCE / JUDGMENT
→ ACTION / REASSESSMENT
```

*Figure 1. The common human-function reasoning spine for internal and external tasks.*

# 7. Functional Capacity, Actual Engagement Process, and Engagement Inference

Human-function capacity answers what the person's body can provide under
specified conditions. In the formal framework, the Actual Engagement
Process is the occurrent, demand-conditioned bodily process through
which required functions actually operate; a Functional Engagement
Assertion is the evidence-bearing representation that binds the person,
demand, required function, context, time, supported process, provenance,
and evidence status [3]. This paper uses “actual engagement” only as
shorthand when those types are clear. Capacity and engagement must
remain distinct because task failure does not prove absent capacity,
task success does not prove low-cost or appropriate engagement, and a
capacity can exist without being effectively organized under the present
demand [1-3].

The simplest governed interpretation uses two primary axes: capacity
coverage and evidence about actual engagement. Capacity may be
sufficient or insufficient relative to the demand. Separately, an
Engagement-Pattern Inference may classify the evidence as appropriate,
compensatory, limited, not established, undetermined, or another
governed value [3]. These are epistemic classifications and must not
be encoded as if they were the Actual Engagement Process itself.
Coexisting capacity and engagement problems are possible. Evidence
insufficiency is not a third biological problem type; it is an epistemic
state.

This distinction creates the functional bridge between biological state
and lived reality. Domain-specific physiological or biomechanical models
can constrain the capacity estimate. Real-world observation and repeated
response can inform actual engagement. Neither alone determines the
complete whole-person judgment.

## 7.1 The UOHF human-function capacity coordinate system

HFWM requires a governed coordinate system for stating what stable
human-function capacities the whole person has before those capacities
are bound to a specific demand. The current public UOHF Version 1.0
capacity catalogue provides 18 CORE_CAPACITY objects and 104 subordinate
specific capacities, formally represented as SUBCAPACITY and
CAPACITY_COMPONENT where applicable [12]. These are whole-person
capacities, not anatomical structures, physiological processes, tasks,
one-time performances, measurements, or state labels. The 18 + 104
result is versioned and auditable rather than an immutable claim that no
future capacity can be added.

Within HFWM, this catalogue is a coordinate set rather than a fixed
checklist. A governed INTERNAL_TASK or EXTERNAL_TASK makes its Demand
explicit; the Demand identifies a task-specific subset of required
capacities; person-specific evidence then constrains Current Capacity
and the Actual Engagement Process. The catalogue therefore answers what
stable human-function capacities can be referenced, while HFWM answers
which capacities matter now, whether they are available, how they
actually operate, and how those facts change over time.

# 8. Structure, Physiology, Disease, Injury, and Recovery Inside HFWM

HFWM does not place anatomy, physiology, disease, or recovery outside
the model. Bodily structures provide realization and constraint bases;
physiological processes realize, regulate, and modify capacities;
disease and injury alter internal conditions and safety; recovery
processes change future availability and boundaries. What HFWM prevents
is category collapse: a structure is not a capacity, a physiological
process is not automatically a whole-person function, a disease is not a
task, a manifestation is not a cause, and a measurement result is not a
complete state.

A whole-person state transition can therefore include physiological
transition and human-function transition simultaneously. An intervention
may change inflammation, perfusion, sleep, or tissue repair while also
changing the person's ability to stand, eat, communicate, work, train,
or recover. The model should preserve both scales and the explicit
relations between them.

Self-healing is not represented as one opaque outcome or as one
undifferentiated internal task. Where canonical internal-task identities
and governed trigger rules are available, HFWM can represent
self-healing and recovery through multiple internal tasks such as
defense, hemostasis, regulation, repair, restoration, recovery, and
adaptation; the Demand generated by each task; the required
human-function capacities; and the Actual Engagement Processes through
which those capacities operate. This does not require every
physiological process to be mechanically rewritten as a Task.
Longitudinal observed responses and state changes together characterize
whether self-healing and recovery are progressing, incomplete, or
failing. This gives physiological change a human-function meaning
without reducing physiology to human function.

## 8.1 Functional Bridge as a governed knowledge-relation layer

A whole-person world model also needs explanatory knowledge that
connects stable human-function capacities to anatomical structures,
spatial organization, physiological and biological processes,
cross-capacity organization, response patterns, state/time/change, and
evidence boundaries through which those capacities may be realized or
constrained. In the current HFWM architecture, this role is served by a
governed knowledge-relation layer termed Functional Bridge.

Functional Bridge is not a new ontology layer, is not an A14 extension
of UOHF, and does not modify the identity, hierarchy, or definitions of
the 18 core and 104 specific capacities. It is also not person-specific
evidence. A bridge proposition can support scientific explanation and
knowledge retrieval, but it cannot by itself establish an individual's
diagnosis, causal attribution, capacity problem, engagement problem, or
action prescription. A proposition considered for formal UOHF status
requires separate ontology governance; a proposition used in
person-specific judgment requires independently admissible evidence and
authority.

## 8.2 HRA and WPP as scientific reference sources

Functional Bridge can use external reference resources as provenance for
explanatory propositions without collapsing those resources into UOHF or
treating general reference knowledge as an individual fact. The Human
Reference Atlas (HRA) provides a peer-reviewed reference framework for
anatomical structures, cell types, biomarkers, and spatial organization
[13]. The NIH Whole Person Reference Physiome (WPP) is building an
integrated map and model of healthy male and female physiology across
systems and scales [5,6]. Within HFWM, HRA-derived statements are used
as anatomical/spatial reference knowledge and WPP-derived statements as
multiscale physiological/mechanistic reference knowledge. Neither source
automatically establishes a formal UOHF relation or a person-specific
causal conclusion.

This paper intentionally limits disclosure to the scientific role and
governance boundary of Functional Bridge. It does not publish private
source-mapping registries, complete relation inventories,
retrieval/reranking logic, coverage ledgers, production prompts,
database schemas, decision weights, or other commercial implementation
details.
