## Summary

Existing frameworks describe functioning, capacity, performance, person–environment relations, and multiscale biological states, but they do not make the demand-conditioned relation among these objects a common person-bound target of inference. We hypothesise that human function—the body’s capacity to be appropriately engaged to meet internal and external demands—can provide such an organising coordinate. The proposed scientific addition is the explicit relation among required capacities, evidence-supported current capacities, their actual engagement, cost, boundaries, recovery, and change over time. One versioned operationalisation maps 18 core and 104 subordinate capacities; a longitudinal extension connects this coordinate across biological, clinical, behavioural, environmental, and temporal scales. The hypothesis does not replace diagnosis, ICF, person–environment models, or physiology, and it is not validated by ontology construction. Its value depends on whether the added relations can be mapped reproducibly and improve prospective explanation or prediction against evidence-matched alternatives.

## The problem is how to reconnect the person

Engel’s challenge to biomedicine concerned the exclusion of psychological, behavioural, and social dimensions of illness.¹ Tinetti and Fried argued for individual goals rather than disease alone as the focus of care; Barnett and colleagues documented the mismatch between multimorbidity and services organised around individual diseases.²˒³ Delgado identified a further problem: richer data can lose meaning when forced into inherited health constructs.⁴ Together, these arguments motivate a question beyond collecting more data: what common object lets different disciplines interpret change in the same person?

The International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health (ICF) distinguishes capacity and performance and includes environmental factors.⁵ WHO’s healthy-ageing framework relates intrinsic capacity, environment, and functional ability.⁶ Person–Environment–Occupation models explain occupational performance through transactions among person, environment, and occupation,⁷ while human functioning science positions functioning between biological and lived health.⁸ These are substantive foundations. Multiscale physiology, precision medicine, patient-level AI, deep phenotyping, digital-organism and digital-twin proposals, and the Whole Person Reference Physiome address increasingly integrated biological and person-specific representations.⁹–¹⁵

These approaches leave a narrower relation insufficiently explicit as a common target of inference: for the same person at a specified time, what capacities are required by a demand, which capacities are currently available, how are those capacities actually engaged, at what cost and within what boundary, and how does this relation change over time? A diagnosis does not answer this; capacity or performance alone does not answer it; a multiscale biological state does not answer it. We hypothesise that human function can provide this demand-conditioned organising coordinate. This is the claimed scientific addition—not a replacement for ICF, person–environment models, functioning science, or physiology.

## A demand-conditioned human-function hypothesis

In the author’s prior preprints, human function is defined as the body’s capacity to be appropriately engaged to meet internal and external demands.¹⁶˒¹⁷ Capacity is a person-bound potential; actual functional engagement is the bodily process through which relevant capacities operate under a specified demand. Unlike ICF performance—what a person does in their environment—engagement denotes that bodily operation, not patient participation or task outcome. The root empirical hypothesis is that preserving the demand–required capacity–current capacity–actual engagement relation as a distinct person-bound object improves reproducible interpretation and prospective explanation or prediction beyond equally informed representations that omit that relation.

The Unified Ontology of Human Function (UOHF) is one formalisation of this hypothesis, not evidence for it. Its current public preprint catalogue provides 18 core capacities and 104 subordinate specific-capacity objects (table 1), including, for example, Force Establishment Capacity and Braking Force Output Capacity within Force Output Capacity.¹⁸ These are semantic coordinates, not independent statistical axes, additive scores, or a mandatory testing checklist. The 18+104 catalogue is not the hypothesis itself; it is a versioned operationalisation of the capacity domain. Its boundaries can be revised without changing the root hypothesis, which stands or falls on the empirical utility of the demand-conditioned relation.

External tasks specify demands under particular conditions. Within this hypothesis, internal demands are requirements generated by internal biological goals or perturbations—for example, maintaining homeostasis or restoring usable capacity after disturbance. A physiological process is not human function merely because it sustains life: circulation, immune responses, tissue-repair processes, or sleep physiology may realise, constrain, or modify capacities, but enter a human-function inference only when related to an explicit demand, a capacity, and evidence of engagement. Internal and external demands can interact. ‘Appropriate’ must be specified before the outcome, using demand requirements, person-specific safety boundaries, acceptable burden, and feasible recovery—not inferred retrospectively from success.¹⁷˒¹⁹

## From coordinates to human-function judgement

The proposed judgement sequence is: identify the person, time, safety constraints, task or internal requirement, and demand conditions; identify required capacities; estimate current capacities from relevant evidence; assess actual engagement separately; interpret response, cost, burden, boundaries, reserve, and recovery; formulate the limiting-factor hypotheses and current state; select a justified priority and authorised action; then record execution, feedback, and reassessment.¹⁷˒¹⁹ This is a reasoning sequence, not a claim that demand creates capacity.

Requirements do not establish deficits. Capacity can be insufficient despite appropriate use of what remains; adequate capacity can coexist with engagement difficulty. Both problems can coexist. Without adequate capacity evidence, engagement adequacy remains unresolved, although task observations can still be recorded. Task failure, pain, or compensation alone cannot identify a cause. Measurements contribute evidence through appropriate assessment models rather than becoming the functional reality itself.¹⁷

For an illustrative stair-descent task, speed, load, steps, and assistance specify demand. Force output, load tolerance, balance, coordination, and sustained endurance become candidate requirements, not automatic impairments. Independent capacity evidence and task-bound observations are needed to distinguish insufficient capacity from altered engagement; handrail use alone does not establish dysfunction.¹⁷˒¹⁹

A demand-bounded realizability criterion asks whether at least one engagement-pattern specification is realisable from current capacities, ex-ante appropriate, reliably accessible, and compatible with an acceptable potential response.¹⁷ This is a proposed formal test, not a validated biological law. Safety gates cannot be averaged away by unrelated strengths; evidence status remains distinct from functional state, and lack of assessment establishes neither normality nor impairment.

## What the coordinate would change

The proposed answer to fragmentation is not another specialty: it is a common object for medicine, rehabilitation, psychology, exercise, and nutrition. Diagnoses retain their medical identity; professionals relate their observations and actions to shared capacities, demands, engagement, and consequences. This addresses Engel’s integration problem and the disease-centred limitations described by Tinetti and Barnett at the representational level; it does not itself solve access, inequality, or service organisation.¹–³

Delgado’s call for new constructs becomes a testable requirement to preserve meaning across heterogeneous records.⁴ Multiscale biological models can inform or constrain capacity estimates, but their outputs do not by themselves establish person-level function or diagnosis. Within the proposed HFWM architecture, a related knowledge-relation layer (Functional Bridge) is intended to connect anatomy and physiology to capacities while keeping general knowledge separate from individual evidence; a known relation does not establish an individual cause.¹⁹ Values, preferences, and relationships are retained; personhood is not reduced to functional utility.

Disease and treatment can alter capacity, engagement, and recovery; functional change can inform goals, outcomes, and medical reassessment. Medical clearance alone does not necessarily establish full functional recovery, and apparently preserved function does not exclude medical risk. Shared coordinates do not confer shared professional authority.⁵˒¹⁷˒¹⁹

## A longitudinal multiscale extension

The Human Function World Model (HFWM) is proposed only as a longitudinal multiscale extension of the root hypothesis, not as a second hypothesis or proof of the first. It keeps the same person as the modeled entity across molecules, cells, tissues, organs, physiological systems, disease, capacities, engagement, behaviour, tasks, environment, and time.¹⁹ Human function is the organising coordinate, not the boundary of biology; physiological and domain-specific models may contribute through explicit mappings that retain provenance and uncertainty.

The empirical claim is deliberately narrower than ‘simulate the whole human’: a longitudinal person model that preserves demand–capacity–engagement relations, history, action or exposure, and feedback should add calibrated explanatory or predictive value over evidence-matched alternatives that omit those relations.¹⁹ Model revision must remain distinguishable from biological change in the person.

## How to test and potentially refute the hypothesis

A first study should compare independent capacity assessments with task-bound engagement observations across controlled changes in demand or context. Capacity stability must be checked, not assumed. Repeated failure to distinguish the proposed targets under adequate measurement would weaken their empirical utility. Matching tested ability to demand already has precedents; the test is incremental value, not renaming.²⁰

Second, independent teams should map the same cross-professional cases using the proposed coordinate and evidence-matched existing frameworks, including ICF or person–environment approaches. Blinded adjudication should assess agreement, preserved provenance, unsupported attribution, and information loss without presuming the proposed labels are correct. This tests the common-coordinate claim rather than software functionality.

Third, prospective evaluation should compare the longitudinal multiscale extension with simpler baselines and evidence-matched multimodal alternatives. Prespecify outcomes, such as recovery time or subsequent task tolerance, and meaningful improvement thresholds. Use comparable modelling capacity and held-out participants; prevent future responses from leaking into predictors. Ablations should test demand binding, engagement, and cross-scale relations separately. Added complexity requires reproducible gains in calibration, prediction, or decision usefulness; predictive gains alone do not establish treatment benefit.

Failure of a particular catalogue boundary, mapping, judgement rule, or HFWM implementation should revise that operationalisation. Repeated failure of the core demand-conditioned capacity–engagement relation to add reproducible interpretive or predictive value across independent implementations and domains would challenge the root hypothesis itself. Clinical benefit requires separate prospective evaluation; constructing UOHF or HFWM is not proof of it.
