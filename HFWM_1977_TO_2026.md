# From 1977 to 2026: The Human Function World Model Is Responding to a Question Medicine Has Pursued for Nearly 50 Years

In 1977, George Engel published **The Need for a New Medical Model** in *Science*, challenging a biomedical model that was too narrowly centered on disease and biological abnormality.

Forty-nine years later, medicine has genomics, proteomics, medical imaging, electronic health records, wearable sensing, artificial intelligence and digital twins. We can observe more and more parts of the human body in extraordinary detail, yet a basic question remains:

> **How do disease, structure, physiology, psychology, behavior, everyday life and change over time become part of a continuous representation of the same person?**

Over nearly five decades, major lines of research have approached different parts of this problem.

## From disease back to the whole person

Engel argued that medicine could not understand people through disease and biology alone.

In 2002, Denis Noble pushed the problem further in *Science*: physiological function cannot be understood from genes, proteins or isolated organs alone. It requires the functional interactions that span subcellular, cellular, tissue, organ and system levels.

In other words:

> **Knowing what the body is made of is not the same as knowing how the body works.**

In 2015, Francis Collins and Harold Varmus described the Precision Medicine Initiative in *NEJM*, bringing individual variation and high-dimensional personal data closer to the center of medicine.

In parallel, WHO frameworks elevated **intrinsic capacity** and **functional ability** as central concepts in health. The medical question increasingly extends beyond “What disease does this person have?” toward “What does this disease mean for this person, and what can this person still do?”

In 2022, Jane Delgado argued in *NEJM* that new health data cannot simply be forced into old health constructs. In 2025, Ziad Obermeyer discussed how AI may allow medicine to reason from patient-level data back toward mechanisms and theory.

The bottleneck is therefore shifting from:

**Do we have enough data?**

to:

> **How should we represent a person?**

## WPP, VHT and digital-human research are turning the question into engineering

This transition is no longer confined to conceptual papers.

The NIH-funded **Whole Person Physiome (WPP)** is working toward a cross-organ and cross-system representation of healthy human physiology, with shared data, models and tools.

The European **Virtual Human Twins (VHT) Initiative** is developing infrastructure for virtual human models that can be integrated, combined and validated across scales.

In 2026, *Nature Medicine* pushed the frontier further.

Le Song, Eran Segal and Eric Xing proposed an **AI-driven digital organism (AIDO)** built from integrated multiscale foundation models spanning molecules, cells and individuals.

Soon afterward, **A global digital navigator of human health for precision medicine** described trajectory inference for an individual digital twin.

The direction is becoming increasingly clear:

**Whole Person → Multiscale → Longitudinal → Digital Twin → AI Model**

We are beginning to build computational representations of a digital person.

## But one layer remains: what makes all of this the same person?

Today, one person can generate molecular data, cellular data, organ-level data, physiological measurements, diagnoses, imaging, sleep data, exercise data, nutrition data, psychological and behavioral information, environmental context and medical records.

Putting all of these data in one database does not by itself create a model of the person.

A persistent set of questions remains:

- What can the person still do after disease or injury?
- What demands are internal physiology and real life placing on the body?
- Is current capacity sufficient?
- Are available capacities actually being engaged appropriately?
- What cost is being paid to complete the task?
- Where is the boundary?
- How long does recovery take?
- What changes after medicine, rehabilitation, exercise, nutrition, psychological care or changes in everyday life?

These questions point toward an object that has always existed but has not yet been fully digitized:

# Human function

Human function can be understood simply as:

> **The body’s capacity to be appropriately engaged to meet internal and external demands.**

A Human Function World Model therefore cannot be reduced to a single capacity score. It must represent a chain such as:

> **Demand → Required Capacity → Current Capacity → Actual Engagement → Cost → Boundary → Recovery**

## Human function as an organizing coordinate

HFWM keeps the **same person** as the persistent modeled entity and uses human function as an organizing coordinate connecting disease, injury, structure, physiology, internal tasks, real-world demands, functional capacity, actual engagement, recovery, environment, professional action and longitudinal history.

Connection does not mean collapse.

Disease remains disease. Imaging remains imaging. Medication remains medication. Medical risk remains subject to medical judgment. Human-function state does not become a diagnosis.

The central HFWM hypothesis is:

> **Human function may provide an organising coordinate for the same person across biological, medical and real-world scales.**

HFWM is not proposed as a replacement for medicine, WPP, VHT, digital twins, or physiological and mechanistic models.

It addresses a different layer:

> **How can these distinct domains remain distinct, yet still be organized around the same person?**

## From 1977 to 2026, the question may have reached its next layer

Engel returned medicine to the whole person.

Noble showed why life must be understood across interacting scales.

Precision medicine expanded individual-level data.

WHO elevated functioning and capacity.

WPP is mapping whole-person physiology.

European VHT programs are building multiscale virtual-human infrastructure.

AIDO connects molecules, cells and individuals.

Digital-twin research is beginning to model individual trajectories.

These efforts were not collectively searching for HFWM, and they do not prove that HFWM is correct.

But together they bring us to a sharper question:

> **When we can finally digitize more and more parts of a person, what common coordinate can make those parts computationally belong to the same person?**

The Human Function World Model proposes:

# **Start with human function.**

Human function existed before medicine.

Medicine, rehabilitation, exercise, nutrition, psychology and the growing ecosystem of human-centered tools ultimately act on or around the same person.

The question that has not disappeared over nearly fifty years may therefore be not:

**“How much more data can we collect about a person?”**

but:

# **“How do we reconnect those data into one whole person?”**

**The Human Function World Model is an attempt to answer that question.**
